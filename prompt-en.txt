Prepare a private batch for “Phish & Chips”, a quick phishing-awareness game. Players classify an email as legitimate or phishing, then read a short explanation. The game sounds like a grumpy security colleague, but its lessons must be simple, concrete and fair.

PARAMETERS
- Exactly 20 emails: 10 phishing and 10 legitimate. If I change the total, maintain 50/50 for an even total and scale the family proportions below.
- All strings in English.
- usage defaults to "training"; use "battle" for competition batches. "both" only on explicit request. Battle scenarios must differ from previous training batches.
- Standard bodies: 35–80 words excluding URLs, including a few short 20–35-word emails. Maximum 100 words. For an express batch: 20–40 words, maximum 50.

ABSOLUTE RULE: AN OBSERVABLE TRAP
Every phishing email must have a concrete indicator accessible in the subject, body, sender or game inspection data. The explanation must cite it precisely. The verdict must be defensible without guessing an invisible event. Replace any ambiguous scenario before returning the batch.
Never rely on:
- “The account is compromised”, “a hacker controls this legitimate site”, or “the attachment contains malware” without an observable trap.
- Invisible final redirects, files, headers, SPF/DKIM/DMARC results or post-click pages.
- Knowing an invented company’s official domain without a visible comparison.
- Urgency, a typo, an external domain or a sender mismatch alone. Add a dangerous request or concrete contradiction.
A matching sender can still demand a password, an unsolicited login code, bypassing the established bank-details verification procedure or executing a command to open a document. Base the verdict on the visible request, never an assumed compromise. Simple, easy-to-defeat traps are welcome.

DISTRIBUTION OF THE 10 PHISHING EMAILS (ONE PRIMARY FAMILY EACH)
1. Three link/domain traps: a near-identical address, a misleading subdomain and link text masking another destination. Provide a visible comparison and a sensitive action requested on the wrong destination.
2. Two workplace frauds: a payment/bank-details change explicitly bypassing the usual verification; an unusual confidential-data/gift-card request involving secrecy or bypassing procedure. At least one has no link.
3. Two login abuses: sharing/validating a code without a login initiated by the recipient; granting permissions clearly disproportionate to an application’s stated need. The dangerous request must be visible in the email, not just its explanation.
4. One impersonated display name: an inspectable address contradicts the name and accompanies a sensitive request. A sender/envelope mismatch does not always mean an attack.
5. One hijacked conversation or document-share notification: relevant context is visible, with a clearly incompatible new request. A service’s brand alone cannot establish maliciousness.
6. One fake support request: call the supplied number to disclose a password/code, OR execute a command to “repair” document access (ClickFix). Do not supply a working dangerous command.
Alternate variants, subjects and characters across batches. Do not cram every technique into each scenario.

CONTRAST AND VARIETY
- At least 4 phishing emails have matching, plausible displayed and realSender addresses: the request or link provides the indicator.
- At least 3 phishing emails are calm and correctly written; at least 2 have no link. Constraints can overlap.
- Legitimate emails also cover HR, documents, invoices, delivery, security, office life and personal communications. Do not reserve a topic for phishing.
- At least 2 legitimate emails have ordinary urgency/deadlines and at least 3 contain links consistent with the visible context. No secret disclosure or bypassing procedures.
- Legitimate scenarios must be ordinary, coherent and unambiguous. A login-security notification may explain how to open the known service independently; do not label an unexpected approval demand safe.
- Vary greetings, signatures, length and register. Legitimate emails can be brief or imperfect; phishing can be immaculate.
- Shuffle order: no fixed alternation or blocks by verdict. Length, humour, link count and wording must not automatically reveal answers.
- Subtle office humour is welcome. Explanations may be sharp, but visible facts and useful actions come before jokes. No mandatory compliments.

ENGINE CAPABILITIES AND LIMITS
- Players inspect realSender and data-real-link destinations. realSender models a simulated SMTP envelope, not a guarantee of the author’s identity.
- body is text with JSON "\n" line breaks. Only this link markup is supported: <a href='DESTINATION' data-real-link='DESTINATION'>displayed text</a>.
- href and data-real-link contain the same inspectable destination. Displayed text may be a different URL or a label such as “Open document”. An indicator cannot rely on href alone: players see the text and the inspection destination.
- Fictional names and reserved .test domains. No real personal data, active attack domains, dangerous commands or giveaway paths named “malware” or “stealpassword”.
- No general HTML, images, real attachment cards, QR codes or post-click steps: the engine does not support them. Do not create scenarios whose solution depends on those features.
- Use 2–4 short paragraphs, one main request and at most 2 links. No unfilled placeholders.
- HTTPS, logos, good spelling and a matching sender do not prove safety. A legitimate provider may use another domain. Do not turn those features into absolute rules.

SIMPLE EXPLANATIONS: EXACTLY TWO STRINGS IN clues
For phishing:
1. "What to notice: ..." — name the visible detail and explain why it should raise concern. At most 35 words after the prefix, one or two short sentences.
2. "The right move: ..." — one practical action, at most 25 words after the prefix.
For legitimate emails:
1. "What fits: ..." — a visible fact explaining consistency within this scenario, at most 35 words. No “no indicators, therefore safe” or absolute guarantees.
2. "The right move: ..." — a proportionate action such as reading the information, opening the known service independently when needed or following the usual procedure. At most 25 words.
Avoid jargon: “URL obfuscation”, “TLD”, “harvesting”, “DMARC alignment”. Say where a link leads, what code is requested or which bank-details check is bypassed. Show readable differences rather than merely saying “fake domain”.
Never invent a fact absent from the email/inspection, recommend verification using the number/link from that same message or rely on an invisible compromise.

IMPORT FORMAT
Return only a valid JSON array: no Markdown, comments or surrounding text. Each object contains exactly:
- sender: displayed name and address, "Name <person@domain.test>"; maximum 300 characters.
- realSender: simulated SMTP envelope address; maximum 300 characters.
- subject: maximum 300 characters.
- body: text and simulated links; maximum 6,000 characters.
- type: "phishing" or "safe" (legitimate in this scenario).
- usage: "training" or "battle", according to my request; "both" only on explicit request.
- clues: the two explanation strings, each under 500 characters.
No id, category or verdict in the email body. The server assigns IDs; explanations are not sent before players answer.

PRE-DELIVERY CHECK (DO NOT PRINT)
Verify totals, balance, families, contrast constraints, JSON and limits. For every phishing email, identify the accessible fact justifying its verdict. For each legitimate email, ensure it does not contain a dangerous request labelled phishing elsewhere. Explanations must be short, faithful to accessible evidence, understandable without expertise and paired with a useful action. Rewrite any ambiguous scenario or verdict relying on hidden information.
